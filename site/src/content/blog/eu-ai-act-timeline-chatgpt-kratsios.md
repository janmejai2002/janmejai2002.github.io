## Executive TL;DR

- Michael Kratsios, director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, told Y Combinator's Startup School the administration won't set "firm red lines" on AI because they don't survive contact with a fast-moving frontier.
- His lead example — that the EU AI Act "was passed and finalized before ChatGPT was... ever invented" — doesn't hold up. The political deal was struck in December 2023, over a year after ChatGPT shipped, and generative AI is exactly why the final text was rewritten to cover "general-purpose AI models."
- He sorts technology into two buckets for regulatory purposes: things "born free" (no rules yet, leave them alone) and things "born in captivity" (need a permit to operate, like drones needing FAA sign-off).
- On risk, he called cyber capability from powerful models a live concern but said fears about AI-enabled bioweapons have been "a bit overblown" since 2021–22.
- He confirmed the administration's line on open-weight models — first stated in last year's AI Action Plan — hasn't shifted, despite a week of rumors that an executive order would restrict open source.
- His R&D funding history (federal share of research inverting from roughly 70% to 30% since the 1950s) is directionally right, but the current federal share is closer to 21%, not 30%.

</div>

## Who, where, when

Kratsios spoke on August 18, 2026, at Y Combinator's Startup School, in a sit-down interview with Luther Lowe, YC's Washington-based head of public policy (previously the same role at Yelp — a detail he brings up himself late in the conversation). Kratsios is a Senate-confirmed "assistant to the president," runs OSTP, and served as the country's first CTO in Trump's first term before a stint as Scale AI's COO. The full 40-minute conversation, [Michael Kratsios: Inside the White House's AI Strategy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU), is on YC's channel.

## The argument he's actually making

Strip away the DC color and Kratsios is defending one structural bet: don't legislate AI with hard, specific thresholds, because the frontier moves faster than any threshold can be redrawn once it's law.

Around the 6-minute mark, he anchors this in current events — the "noisy week" just before the talk, when a Y Combinator-organized letter urged the White House not to restrict open-weight models, after rumors of a restrictive executive order. Kratsios says the alarm was overblown: the administration's position was set on "page one" of the AI Action Plan released in July 2025 — a commitment to both open and closed AI ecosystems working together — and hasn't moved. He frames the letter-writing itself as useful, not a sign of policy panic: without noise from industry, he says, "we can't make the best decisions."

By the 9-minute mark he's building the general case against rigid rules, and this is where the EU comes in as his cautionary tale (see below). Around 11 minutes, he applies the same logic to the Biden administration's own AI executive order, which set a specific compute threshold above which developers had to disclose to the government — a rule Kratsios says does "not stand the test of time" and is why his team resists doing the same.

At roughly the 13-minute mark, asked which AI risk is overrated, he names biological risk: real people were "shouting about the bio problem" back in 2021–22, he says, and it "hasn't been an issue for 3 years" — while cyberattack capability, in his telling, is the more live concern right now, one where the same model hardening a system can also attack one.

Around 16 minutes, prompted by Lowe's mention of the newly formed "Little Tech Association" (a YC-backed trade group meant to counterweight the largest AI labs' lobbying), Kratsios argues that a single national AI standard — preempting a state-by-state patchwork — is itself the thing that protects small developers, since only large companies can afford to navigate 50 different rulebooks. Big companies "can deal with that," he says; the room full of founders can't.

By 19 minutes he offers his cleanest framework: technologies are "either born free or born in captivity." Born-free technologies — his example is the early internet — have no rulebook yet, and the job of policy is to leave them alone. Born-in-captivity technologies need a government sign-off to operate commercially at all — his example is a drone startup that can build a working product in a garage but can't legally fly it without an FAA waiver.

The last third of the talk moves away from AI specifically: a long walkthrough of what OSTP actually does day to day (roughly 22 minutes in), a report titled "Science and a New Golden Age" that Kratsios's office released the same week (around 27 minutes), a vision of AI-run "autonomous cloud labs" doing unattended scientific experimentation (30 minutes), a pitch for prioritizing quantum computing the way his office pushed AI in 2019 (32 minutes), and a call for Congress — not just executive orders — to legislate AI preemption and creator IP protections (35 minutes).

## What holds up, and what doesn't

The claim that checks out cleanly: Kratsios says Trump signed "the first executive order in the history of the United States prioritizing artificial intelligence" in February 2019. That's accurate — [Executive Order 13859](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/02/14/2019-02544/maintaining-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence), "Maintaining American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," was signed February 11, 2019, and did launch the American AI Initiative.

The claim that doesn't check out is the one doing the most argumentative work: that the EU AI Act "was passed and finalized before ChatGPT ... was ever invented." ChatGPT launched November 30, 2022. The EU's negotiators didn't reach political agreement on the AI Act until [December 8, 2023](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2023/12/09/artificial-intelligence-act-council-and-parliament-strike-a-deal-on-the-first-worldwide-rules-for-ai/) — more than a year later — and the fight that ran longest in those talks was specifically about how to regulate "general-purpose AI models," the category ChatGPT itself created. The regulation entered into force in mid-2024. It's true the original 2021 Commission proposal predates ChatGPT, and true that early drafts didn't anticipate general-purpose foundation models — that's a fair criticism of legislating too early. But "passed and finalized" before ChatGPT existed reverses the actual sequence: the final law was rewritten because of generative AI's arrival, not oblivious to it.

The R&D history is closer to right than wrong, but overstated. Kratsios says the federal government funded "almost 70%" of national R&D around 1950 and now funds "only about 30%," with the private sector inverting to 70%. NSF's own data puts the federal share at 67% in 1964, falling to roughly [21% by 2020](https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsb20257/trends-in-u-s-r-d-performance-and-funding) — a more dramatic inversion than he described, not a less dramatic one. The direction of his argument (government funds a shrinking slice, private capital and philanthropy fund the rest) is well-supported; the specific number he reached for is off by close to 10 points.

Worth flagging as a matter of category, not accuracy: nearly everything else in the talk — the description of how OSTP coordinates agencies, what "Little Tech" needs, whether open-weight policy has changed — is Kratsios describing his own office's intentions and process. That's testimony about what the administration believes and says it's doing, not an independently verifiable result. It's worth taking at face value as a statement of position, while remembering that a policymaker describing his own policy is not the same kind of claim as an external, checkable fact.

## What it means for you

If you build with AI and you're trying to read Washington's next move, the actionable signal here is narrower than the talk's tone suggests: no change to open-weight policy, a continued push for one national standard over a state patchwork, and a stated aversion to hard compute or capability thresholds in any future rule. That's useful to know. But the rhetorical scaffolding around it — particularly the EU comparison — is doing more persuasive work than the facts support, and it's worth separating "this is our policy" from "and here's proof the alternative failed," because in this case the proof doesn't hold up.
