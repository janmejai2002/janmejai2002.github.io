## What was actually disclosed, and what was just rewritten

The number doing the rounds is that about a quarter of McKinsey's global fees now come from outcome-based pricing. It is real, and the firm has said it on the record. Michael Birshan, a McKinsey managing partner, put it plainly: "We're doing more performance-based arrangements with our clients." A related figure from the same reporting: straight strategy advice is now under 20% of what the firm does.

Two things the rewrites tend to skip. First, outcomes pricing did not start with AI. McKinsey has offered it for years; what changed is that AI transformation work suits it, because the client can point to a number that moved. Second, the pricing shift has a payroll consequence the firm is handling internally. Because an outcome fee is paid later and is less certain than a billed scope, revenue got lumpier, and McKinsey is shifting a larger share of partner pay from cash into equity under an overhaul the Financial Times reported under the codename Project Acorn.

The AI side is not subtle. Lilli, McKinsey's internal assistant, handles more than 500,000 prompts a month, and consultants report up to 30% time savings on knowledge work. In January 2026 Bob Sternfels said the firm was running about 25,000 personalised AI agents alongside its 40,000 people, and that AI had saved roughly 1.5 million hours of research and synthesis in a year. Headcount is down from a peak near 45,000 at the end of 2023. The analyst grind that used to fill a first-year's week is the exact thing being automated, and that is what forces the pricing question.

## Anatomy of an outcomes-based engagement

"Pay for results" is a slogan. The contract is a set of definitions, and each one is negotiable:

- **Baseline.** The number you measure improvement against. Pick the month, the metric, and whether it is seasonally adjusted. A generous baseline makes the consultant's job easier.
- **KPI definition.** "Cost reduction" means what, exactly: gross or net of the fee, one-time or annualised, booked savings or realised savings.
- **Measurement window.** How long after the work you check, and how long the result has to hold. A short window rewards a quick spike; a long one exposes the consultant to your later decisions.
- **Gainshare band.** The consultant's cut of the value created, usually with a floor (a minimum fee regardless) and a cap (a ceiling, so a windfall does not produce an absurd invoice).
- **Clawback.** If the result reverses inside the window, how much comes back.

None of this is exotic. All of it is where the engagement is actually priced, and most of it is invisible in coverage that stops at "the billable hour is dead."

## Where it goes wrong: attribution

The Government Outcomes Lab at Oxford studies outcomes-based contracts in the public sector, where they have a longer track record. Its taxonomy of gaming transfers almost directly to consulting.

- **Cherry-picking:** a provider only accepts outcomes-based terms on engagements it already expects to hit. The hard problems stay on time-and-materials.
- **Creaming:** effort concentrates on "those individuals who are easiest to help," in the Lab's words. In an advisory engagement, that is the metric that moves with least resistance, not the one that matters most.
- **Parking:** quiet neglect of the parts of the mandate that are difficult to move.

Underneath all three is attribution. If revenue rose during the engagement, was it the consultant, the market, a pricing change you had planned anyway, or a competitor stumbling? The Lab's answer is that reliable attribution needs "comparison to a reference group who did not participate," which costs money and time most engagements will not spend. So attribution gets settled by negotiation, and the party with better data and more lawyers tends to win it. "Pay for results, not hours" does not remove the argument. It moves the argument from how many hours to whose result, and the second argument is harder to end.

## The pyramid problem

Consulting economics run on leverage: a few partners, more managers, many analysts. AI is thinning the base of that structure. If a first-year no longer spends a year building models and decks, the firm needs fewer first-years, which is part of what a headcount drop from 45,000 toward 40,000 reflects.

The buyer-side consequence is continuity. The apprenticeship that turned analysts into the managers and partners you actually want on your account ran on exactly the work AI now absorbs. Firms will say AI plus judgement is a fair substitute for that training. Maybe. But if you are signing a multi-year engagement, it is worth asking who is being trained on your account, and whether the person you trust will still be there in year three.

## The India cut

The same logic is running through Indian IT services, and the vocabulary is the same even though the work is not. In August 2026 Business Standard reported a "small uptick in outcome-based pricing deals, a shift away from the traditional time and material (T&M) and fixed price ones."

Be careful reading it as a wave. Infosys CEO Salil Parekh said clients are more interested but "it is not so much that it has become a large part of our activity." An "outcome" in a managed-services contract, meaning tickets closed, uptime held, a migration delivered, is a tighter and more measurable thing than an "outcome" in a strategy engagement, so the attribution problem is smaller there. That also makes it less of a change: an outcome-based managed-services deal can look a lot like a service-level agreement with a new label.

For context on how far the AI reweighting goes at the strategy firms: Bain reports AI and tech-enabled work at roughly 30% of its consulting business and expects that toward 50%, and in May 2026 it announced an OpenAI partnership and investment. Around 150 former McKinsey, Bain and BCG consultants have reportedly been hired to train AI models on entry-level consulting tasks.

## If you are the buyer

- **Match the pricing to the engagement, not the trend.** Outcomes pricing fits work with a clean, attributable metric and a measurement window you can both accept. It fits diagnostics, open-ended strategy, and work where you will change major decisions mid-engagement much less well. For those, a scoped fee is the right structure.
- **Negotiate the measurement clause as hard as the rate.** Baseline, KPI definition, window, cap, floor, clawback. That is the real price.
- **Ask what the firm's own AI use is doing to the bill.** If a deliverable now takes 30% less time, a fee based on the old effort is a price increase in disguise.
- **Pre-register the metric, and a comparison if you can afford one.** Even a rough control, one region held back or one business unit left unchanged, makes the year-end attribution conversation shorter.
- **Name who is on the account and who is being trained on it.** Continuity is a term you can negotiate before it becomes a complaint.

## FAQ

**Is the billable hour actually dead?**

No. It is losing share at the top firms for AI-heavy transformation work. Time-and-materials and fixed-fee are still the right structure for a lot of advisory, and outcomes pricing sits alongside them, not on top.

**Does AI make consulting cheaper?**

Not automatically. If the fee is tied to a result rather than hours, faster delivery does not lower the price. It can raise the firm's margin while your invoice stays flat, which is one reason to ask about AI use directly.

**How do you measure an outcome you can't fully attribute?**

You negotiate a proxy and a window, and ideally a comparison group. If none of those is possible, the outcome is probably not a good candidate for outcomes pricing.

**What am I paying for once the analysis is automated?**

Judgement, accountability, an outside name your board will accept, and sometimes proprietary data or models. AI compresses the research and the first draft. It does not compress the part where someone signs their name to the recommendation.

## A decision rule

Put an engagement on outcomes pricing when the result is measurable, attributable, and lands inside a window you can both live with. Keep it on a scoped fee when the value is judgement, the metric is noisy, or you will change direction halfway through. Whichever you choose, spend your negotiating effort on the measurement clause, because that is where the price actually sits. The billable hour was easy to attack because it was easy to read. Its replacement is neither.
