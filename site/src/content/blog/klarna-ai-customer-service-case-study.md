## Executive TL;DR

- Klarna's OpenAI-built assistant handled 2.3 million customer service conversations in its first month live, which Klarna said matched the output of 700 full-time agents ([Klarna Press Release](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/)).
- Klarna reported average resolution time fell from 11 minutes to under 2 minutes, and repeat inquiries dropped 25% — neither figure was accompanied by an audited measurement method.
- Klarna projected the assistant would contribute a $40 million improvement to its 2024 profit — a forward-looking company projection, not an audited GAAP result.
- In May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski acknowledged in interviews that Klarna had over-rotated toward cost reduction and was bringing human support back to restore quality.
- Evidence grade: **C** for the 2024 launch numbers (first-party, specific, unverified methodology), **D** for the 2025 reversal (reported through press interviews).
- The real story isn't "AI failed at Klarna" — it's that Klarna sequenced cost savings before quality controls, then had to correct course in public.

## The Situation

Klarna is a Swedish fintech best known for buy-now-pay-later checkout, operating across roughly 45 markets with a customer base in the tens of millions. Like most consumer fintechs, it runs a large customer service operation handling refunds, disputes, subscription questions, and delivery issues, historically staffed by a mix of in-house agents and outsourced vendors across many languages.

Heading into 2024, Klarna was under two pressures at once: a well-documented cost-cutting drive following its 2022 valuation drop, and a public push from CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski to position the company as an AI-forward business ahead of an eventual IPO. Customer service — high volume, repetitive, multilingual — was the obvious first target for automation, and Klarna partnered with OpenAI to build it.

## What They Built (Mechanism)

Klarna's assistant is a conversational AI embedded directly in the Klarna app, built on OpenAI's models and trained on Klarna's own support content, policies, and historical chat transcripts. According to Klarna's February 2024 announcement, the assistant went live company-wide after a one-month internal test and was handling customer conversations across 23 markets in more than 35 languages.

The stated scope of the assistant's work, per Klarna: tracking and refunding orders, handling returns, adjusting subscriptions, resolving payment disputes, and answering general account questions — the bulk of Klarna's routine service volume. Klarna described a tiered structure in which the AI resolves what it can and escalates unresolved or sensitive cases to human agents, rather than replacing human support entirely at launch.

## What They Claim

Klarna's launch release provided specific operational metrics:

- 2.3 million conversations in the first month — roughly two-thirds of all customer service chats across the company.
- The work of "700 full-time agents" — the headline figure that was quoted widely across tech and mainstream business press.
- Resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to under 2 minutes, while customer satisfaction ratings remained on par with human agents.
- A 25% drop in repeat inquiries, framed as evidence that issues were being resolved correctly on first contact rather than abandoned.
- An estimated $40 million profit improvement for Klarna in 2024 as a direct result of the deployment.

## How Much to Believe It (Auditing the Numbers)

Start with what's verifiable: Klarna definitely deployed an OpenAI-powered chatbot at massive scale, and that bot handled millions of conversations. The volume claims are consistent with what a company of Klarna's size would expect from routing routine account queries through an in-app prompt.

The softer claims are the ones that drove the headlines:

- **"Work of 700 full-time agents."** This is an input metric framed as an output achievement. It compares the raw number of chats the bot touched against the average chat volume a human agent closes in a month. It does not measure whether those chats delivered the same outcome, how many required follow-up through other channels, or how many users simply gave up on an unhelpful automated response.
- **The 25% drop in repeat inquiries.** Klarna did not disclose the measurement window, how it controlled for seasonal volume variation, or whether it tracked users who switched from chat to email or phone after hitting an AI dead end.
- **The $40 million profit improvement.** This was a company projection published one month into deployment, not an audited accounting result.

The post-launch reality caught up with the narrative in 2025. In press interviews, Siemiatkowski described bringing human agents back into customer support, saying that Klarna had prioritized cost efficiency over customer experience and was actively re-balancing its staffing. The 700-agent headline was quietly retired.

## What You Could Apply

If you're evaluating customer-service automation for your own business, the Klarna trajectory gives you a clear playbook for what to do — and what to avoid:

1. **Build the escalation path before you need it.** Klarna's model always included human escalation for unresolved cases; the reported quality problems emerged from how aggressively that escalation threshold was tuned to minimize human involvement, not from the presence of AI itself.
2. **Expect the correction, and plan for it publicly.** Siemiatkowski's willingness to say on record that Klarna over-rotated toward AI-only cost cutting is the most credible data point in this case study — admitting a deployment mistake is harder to fake than a launch-week statistic. Build a quality review cadence that catches issues early.
3. **Treat "AI did the work of N employees" claims skeptically**, whether in vendor pitch decks or PR releases. It collapses volume handled and quality delivered into a single misleading number.

Klarna's assistant isn't a cautionary tale about AI customer service failing. It's a case study in what happens when the loudest, most precise numbers are the launch numbers, and the correction has to be pieced together from a CEO's press interview more than a year later.
