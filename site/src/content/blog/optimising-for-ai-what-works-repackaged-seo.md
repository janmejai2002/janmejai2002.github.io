## Executive TL;DR

- "Optimising for AI" — sold as GEO, AEO or LLMO — is mostly standard SEO work with new monitoring attached. The genuinely new part is narrow: tracking whether models cite you, and writing content to be quotable inside a synthesised answer rather than clicked.
- The one controlled study the industry cites is from Princeton researchers, published at KDD 2024. Adding citations, quotations and statistics to a page raised its visibility in AI answers by 30 to 40 percent on their metric — tested on a 10,000-query benchmark against GPT-3.5 and Perplexity, not on any real sales funnel.
- In the same study, keyword stuffing produced "little to no improvement", and lower-ranked pages gained most: a page sitting fifth in search results saw a 115 percent visibility jump from adding sources.
- The repeated "AI traffic converts 23x better" figure comes from a single Ahrefs blog post in June 2025, measuring Ahrefs' own site — 0.5 percent of visits produced 12.1 percent of signups. A real result for one B2B software company, not a market-wide rule.
- Gartner's "search volume will fall 25 percent by 2026" is a real February 2024 forecast. It has not happened; Google still holds roughly 90 percent of the search market.
- AI-visibility tools, priced from about $29 to $500 a month, work by running a fixed prompt set through several models on a schedule and tallying mentions. That is a sample, and it shifts with prompt wording, model version and date.

## What the vendors are selling, and what the words mean

Three acronyms, one idea. Generative Engine Optimisation (GEO), Answer Engine Optimisation (AEO) and Large Language Model Optimisation (LLMO) all describe the same goal: getting your brand named, quoted or linked when someone asks ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini or Google's AI Overviews a question your business could answer.

The naming is a turf marker, not a distinction. Google's Danny Sullivan, speaking at WordCamp US in August 2025, put it flatly: "Good SEO is good GEO, or AEO, AIO, LLM SEO, or LMNOPO." Take the point about the acronyms and move to the part that matters, which is whether any specific tactic changes what a model says.

## The evidence, ranked

**Tier one: one controlled study.** In 2023, researchers at Princeton and collaborators published a paper introducing "Generative Engine Optimization" as a term and testing it properly. They built GEO-bench, "a benchmark consisting of 10000 queries from diverse domains", and measured how nine content changes affected a page's visibility in AI-generated answers, using GPT-3.5-turbo as the main test engine and Perplexity as a live one. The paper was accepted to KDD 2024.

Their headline: GEO "can boost visibility by up to 40% in generative engine responses". The specifics are more useful than the headline. The three methods that worked — "Cite Sources, Quotation Addition, and Statistics Addition" — "achieved a relative improvement of 30-40% on the Position-Adjusted Word Count metric". Adding credible citations, direct quotes from authorities, and concrete statistics to a page made AI engines pull from it more.

Two findings from the same paper deserve as much airtime as the 40 percent. Keyword stuffing, the reflex move, offered "little to no improvement". And the gains were uneven: lower-ranked pages benefited far more, with a page ranked fifth in search results seeing a "115.1% increase in visibility" from the Cite Sources method alone. If you are already the top result, there is less on the table.

What this study is not: a measurement of leads, revenue, or even live traffic. It is a controlled test of content features against a benchmark and two engines, at one point in time, on models that have since been replaced. Treat it as a directional signal about content quality, not a guaranteed return.

**Tier two: correlational and platform-confirmed.** Being mentioned on third-party pages, in forums, and on Reddit correlates with being cited by some engines, and a few platforms have confirmed they lean on such sources. This is weaker evidence — you cannot easily isolate the effect — but the tactics (get quoted elsewhere, earn mentions, keep your facts consistent across the web) are cheap and were good practice already.

**Tier three: asserted, not shown.** "Key takeaways" boxes, specific formatting templates, and llms.txt files fall here. On llms.txt in particular, Google has been explicit: it "is not needed for AI Overviews, AI Mode, or any other generative AI Search feature", and John Mueller has compared it to the long-dead keywords meta tag. It costs little to add and does no harm; just do not expect it to move anything in Google's AI surfaces.

## Where the big numbers come from

**"AI traffic converts 23x better."** One source: an Ahrefs blog post from 16 June 2025, titled, in full, "Does AI Search Traffic Convert Better Than Traditional Search? For Ahrefs, Yes: 0.5% of Visitors Drove 12.1% of Signups". Ahrefs looked at twelve months of its own website analytics and found that the small slice of visitors arriving from AI tools signed up at roughly 23 times the rate of organic search visitors. It is a real, honestly labelled result — the title says "For Ahrefs" — for a single B2B SaaS company with a product that a ChatGPT user researching SEO tools is primed to buy. It is not evidence that AI referrals convert 23x better for a law firm, a D2C brand, or a local service business.

**"Up to 40 percent more visible."** The Princeton number, covered above. Controlled benchmark, not a funnel.

**"Gartner says search will drop 25 percent."** This one is real and often mis-stated. Gartner predicted in February 2024 that "traditional search engine volume will drop 25%" by 2026 as people shift queries to AI assistants. Two years on, that has not happened: analyses tracking the period note that "traditional search volume hasn't dropped 25%" and that "Google still commands over 90% of the search market". A separate Gartner figure that circulates, a "35 percent more visible" claim, could not be traced to a published Gartner report and should be treated as unverified.

The pattern across all three: a narrow or forward-looking claim gets repeated until it sounds like a settled category fact. When a vendor quotes one, the useful question is who it was measured on, by whom, and when.

## How AI-visibility tools produce their metrics

The tools — Profound, Peec AI, Otterly, Scrunch and others — do something straightforward. You give them a list of prompts a customer might type. On a schedule, they send those prompts through several models, read the answers, and count: how often your brand is mentioned, whether you are cited with a link, and whether the tone is positive.

That is a survey, not a census. The output depends on which prompts you picked, which model versions ran that day, and when the check happened. Change the prompt wording and the numbers change. A model update can move your "visibility score" with nothing on your site having changed. The tools are useful as a rough trend line — are we mentioned more or less this month — but a precise-looking score carries more uncertainty than its dashboard suggests.

Pricing runs from about $29 a month for an entry tool (Otterly's lowest tier) to the $400 to $500 range for the better-known dashboards; Peec's self-serve plans top out near $495. Profound does not publish prices, and third-party trackers place it between $99 and $399 a month depending on tier. In rupee terms that range runs from roughly ₹2,500 a month at the low end to ₹40,000-plus for the mid-tier dashboards, which for many Indian businesses is a real line item to justify against a channel that is still a rounding error in their referral traffic.

## What to do this quarter with no budget

A measurement setup you can build in an afternoon:

- In your analytics, add a segment for referrals from `chatgpt.com`, `perplexity.ai` and `gemini.google.com`. See what that traffic is actually doing — how much of it there is, which pages it lands on, whether any of it converts.
- Write a list of 20 questions a prospect might ask an AI assistant in your category. Once a month, run them through ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini yourself. Record whether you are mentioned, whether a competitor is, and which sources get cited. Log the date and the model.
- Watch assisted conversions, not just last click. AI answers often sit early in a research journey; the visit that converts may come later from another channel.

Tactics with evidence behind them, from the study above:

- Put real citations, direct quotes and specific numbers into your key pages.
- Keep your core facts — what you do, who you serve, how pricing is structured — consistent across your own site and third-party profiles, so a model synthesising from several sources does not get a muddled picture.
- Earn mentions on pages that already rank and get cited.

Skip, or do only because they are cheap: llms.txt, "key takeaways" boxes added purely for machines, and any tactic a vendor cannot point to evidence for.

## Buy, build, or wait

A rough decision rule:

- **Wait** if AI referrers send you under 1 percent of traffic and you have no way to act on a visibility report. Set up the free measurement above and revisit in a quarter.
- **Build** — train your existing SEO person — if AI traffic is growing but small, and your content team can absorb "add citations and quotes" as a checklist item. This is the right answer for most mid-sized businesses.
- **Buy a tool** if AI referrals are already a meaningful share of pipeline, or you are in a category where being the recommended option matters commercially and you need a monitored trend line to report upward.
- **Hire an agency** only if you would also hire an agency for regular SEO and want one team to cover both. There is no separate GEO expertise worth a separate retainer today; the tactics are public and short.

## FAQ

**Is GEO just SEO with a new invoice?**

Mostly. Authority, structured content, being quoted elsewhere and clean crawlability were SEO fundamentals before the acronym. The new work is prompt-set monitoring and writing to be quoted inside an answer rather than clicked. That does not need a new discipline or a new retainer.

**Is llms.txt worth implementing?**

It is low cost and does no harm, so add it if it takes ten minutes. Do not expect it to affect Google's AI features; Google has said it does not use it.

**Can anyone guarantee my site gets cited by ChatGPT?**

No. The engines change ranking behaviour without notice, and citation is influenced by factors outside your site. Anyone guaranteeing placement is selling certainty they do not have.

**Does AI referral traffic convert well?**

For some businesses, clearly yes — the Ahrefs case is real. Whether it holds for you depends on your category and buyer. Measure your own referral segment before assuming either way.

## The bottom line

Two or three moves have evidence behind them: citations and statistics on your important pages, consistent facts across the web, and mentions on sources that already get cited. Everything else in the "optimising for AI" pitch is either standard SEO with a markup or a claim waiting for proof. Set up the free measurement, give it a quarter, and let your own numbers, not a vendor's slide, decide whether this earns a budget line.
