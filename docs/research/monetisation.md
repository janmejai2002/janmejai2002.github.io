# Monetisation research for wAIbi-sabi

**Scope:** wAIbi-sabi (janmejai2002.github.io) is a static Astro blog on GitHub Pages, 7 published articles about applied AI, written by an automated Claude pipeline with human approval. No analytics installed, effectively zero measured audience today. Owner is a technical individual in India with no budget for paid tools, and an ambition for AI companies to eventually sponsor or showcase products on the blog. The differentiator is radical honesty: the blog grades its own evidence, publishes corrections, and documents its own automation in public.

This document is a skeptical, source-linked survey of what monetisation actually looks like for a blog in this position, what infrastructure fits the constraints, and what to build first. Compiled August 2026. Every claim below carries its source; where a claim is speculative (nobody has real data yet), it is flagged as such.

---

## 1. What actually works for monetising independent technical blogs/newsletters in 2026

There are five real models in play for a solo/small technical publication. In order of how much audience they require before they generate meaningful money:

### 1.1 Sponsorship (in-content or in-newsletter ad slots)

This is the dominant model for AI/dev newsletters specifically, but it needs real reach before it pays real money.

- **CPM ranges for AI/dev audiences (2026):** General tech & developer-tools newsletters command **$60–$150 CPM**; AI-focused newsletters with 30,000+ subscribers are quoting **$140–$220 CPM** because sponsor demand for AI audiences currently outstrips inventory. General newsletter CPMs across niches run **$20–$130 per 1,000 subscribers**. ([Dupple CPM benchmarks](https://dupple.com/learn/cpm-benchmarks-b2b-newsletters), via [WebSearch summary above])
- **Flat-fee reality for small lists:** Newsletters under 5,000 subscribers typically get **$50–$250 per placement** flat fee (not CPM); 5,000–50,000 subscriber lists get **$500–$3,000**; developer-specific example: React Digest charges **$500–$3,000 per placement**, most deals landing $1,000–$1,500. ([Paved: Newsletter Sponsorship Rates](https://www.paved.com/blog/newsletter-sponsorship-rates/), [Newsletter Sponsorship Rates 2026 pricing guide](https://sponsorgap.com/blog/newsletter-sponsorship-rates-2026))
- **Minimum audience before sponsors engage at all:** The rule-of-thumb floor cited repeatedly is **~1,000 engaged subscribers**, but this is soft — creators have landed first sponsors with a few hundred readers if the niche match and engagement (open rate, CTR) are unusually tight. Sponsors buy *the right* audience, not the biggest one. ([beehiiv: How To Book Your First Newsletter Sponsor](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/how-to-book-your-first-newsletter-sponsor-even-with-a-small-list-f2d2), [sponsorgap: How to Get Your First Newsletter Sponsor](https://sponsorgap.com/blog/how-to-get-your-first-newsletter-sponsor))
- **Developer-focused programmatic ad network as a low-audience fallback:** EthicalAds — a no-tracking, contextually-targeted ad network built specifically for developer blogs, docs sites, and technical content — pays roughly **$2.50 CPM** for US/EU traffic and a **70% publisher revenue share**, with no minimum subscriber count required, just approval based on content topic and traffic quality. It serves 35M+ developer impressions/month across docs sites and tech blogs. This is a "something is better than nothing while waiting for direct sponsors" option, not a real income source at low traffic. ([EthicalAds for Publishers](https://www.ethicalads.io/publishers/), [EthicalAds AI publishers page](https://www.ethicalads.io/publishers/ai/))

Skeptical read: at wAIbi-sabi's current audience (effectively zero), sponsorship revenue is not a near-term line item under any of these numbers — even the low end assumes hundreds to low-thousands of engaged subscribers, which the blog does not have.

### 1.2 Reader-funded memberships/subscriptions (Ghost, Patreon, GitHub Sponsors, paid Substack tiers)

- **Fee structures (2026):** Ghost charges **0% platform fee** (self-host or Ghost(Pro) hosting fee only, no revenue cut); Patreon charges **5% (Lite) / 8% (Pro) / 12% (Premium)** plus ~3–5% payment processing; GitHub Sponsors takes **no platform cut**, only unavoidable payment-processor fees. ([Ghost vs Patreon comparison](https://zotopie.com/compare/ghost-vs-patreon/), [Patreon vs GitHub Sponsors](https://www.saashub.com/compare-patreon-vs-github-sponsors))
- **Fit:** GitHub Sponsors is designed for open-source/developer work specifically, not general blog readership — it fits if the blog ships code/tools people want to fund, less so for pure written content. Patreon fits creators with an existing engaged fanbase (podcasters, YouTubers) more than a technical blog. Ghost fits a "publication with a paid tier" model but needs enough readers willing to pay before it's worth the setup.
- **This model requires *trust and habitual readership* before it converts — it is a mid-to-late-stage lever, not a starting one.**

### 1.3 Affiliate

Not deeply researched here because it fits poorly with the blog's radical-honesty positioning (affiliate income creates exactly the incentive-alignment problem the blog is trying to avoid — see Section 4). Worth noting for completeness: affiliate schemes (e.g., Amazon Associates, SaaS affiliate programs) pay on conversions, not audience size, so they can technically work at low traffic, but they require dense product-recommendation content, which is not this blog's format.

### 1.4 Consulting/speaking funnel (blog as top-of-funnel for paid services)

This is the single model that has demonstrably worked for a **solo AI/technical writer with a modest but high-trust audience**, independent of subscriber count:

- **Simon Willison** (35,000+ newsletter subscribers) monetises via a stack of small revenue lines rather than one big one: a weekly sponsor banner on the blog/newsletter (with sponsors having zero editorial influence), occasional EthicalAds placements, X/Twitter Creator Revenue Sharing, GitHub Sponsors for his open-source tools (Datasette etc.), **and ad-hoc consulting/training for companies** — the consulting is explicitly enabled by the credibility the blog built, not the raw traffic number. ([Simon Willison's Newsletter](https://newsletterinsights.io/newsletter/simonw), [sponsorship policy note, Feb 2026](https://newsletterinsights.io/newsletter/simonw))
- Skeptical read: this is the most realistic model for a solo technical writer without paid-growth budget, because it monetises *credibility* rather than *reach* — and credibility is the one asset radical-honesty positioning is specifically built to produce. But it requires the owner personally selling consulting time, which is a different business than "the blog earns passive sponsor revenue."

### 1.5 Bottom line on what "works"

Across all models, **audience size is the gate for sponsorship and membership; credibility/niche fit is the gate for consulting and small-ticket affiliate/ad-network income.** A zero-audience blog cannot access model 1.1 or the sponsor-side of 1.2 in the near term at any meaningful revenue level. It *can* pursue 1.4-style consulting credibility from day one, and can install the free/near-free infrastructure for 1.1 and 1.2 now so it's ready when/if audience arrives.

There is no example here of a **zero-to-low-audience** technical blog earning real sponsorship money quickly; the honest takeaway is that sponsorship is a late-stage reward, not an early-stage plan.

## 3. Realistic sequencing for a zero-audience blog

### 3.1 What milestones actually matter, in order

1. **Publishing cadence and archive depth** — before any traffic strategy works, there needs to be enough content for a reader/crawler/AI assistant to find something worth citing. 7 articles is a reasonable start but thin; case studies above all had sustained weekly or near-weekly output for months to years before monetising.
2. **Any traffic signal at all (pageviews, referrers)** — this is the first thing to instrument, because right now the blog is flying blind. This is a near-zero-cost fix (see Section 6) and should happen before anything else on this list.
3. **Citations/mentions** (being linked from Hacker News, being referenced by another blog, appearing in an AI assistant's answer, ranking for a query) — for a technical/AI blog, this is arguably a more meaningful early signal than raw pageviews, because it's evidence the content is *useful to someone else*, which is the precondition for word-of-mouth growth. No direct case study found of a technical blog tracking this explicitly as a KPI, but it maps to how Simon Willison's and Stratechery's early growth is generally described (peer citation and word of mouth preceding subscriber growth).
4. **Email list / subscriber count** — subscribers matter more than pageviews for monetisation because sponsorship deals are priced per-subscriber, not per-pageview (see Section 1.1 CPM figures, all denominated in subscribers). A blog with high pageviews but no capture mechanism has nothing to sell.
5. **Engagement quality (open rate, CTR) once there is a list** — Ben's Bites' 45% open rate / 5.5% CTR was cited *specifically* as what made its (still-six-figure) list attractive to sponsors, implying quality metrics matter alongside size once a list exists. ([Cost of sponsorships across various newsletters](https://medium.com/@thenewsletternewsletter/cost-of-sponsorships-across-various-newsletters-22fe80bb561a))
6. **First sponsor conversation** — realistically gated on steps 1–5 reaching at least "low hundreds of genuinely engaged subscribers in a legible niche," per the soft floor in Section 1.1.

### 3.2 Timeline reality check

- General blogging-growth sources suggest **reaching 10,000 email subscribers organically typically takes 2–3 years from zero**, with viral/paid tactics being the only way to compress this, and those tactics cost money or an existing following this blog doesn't have. ([Quicksprout: 5 Stages of Blog Growth](https://www.quicksprout.com/5-stages-of-blog-growth-and-traffic-tactics/))
- Take "grow to 1,000 subscribers in 30 days" claims (common in growth-marketing content) with real skepticism — they generally assume an existing audience being redirected (a large social following, a paid ad budget, or a partnership), none of which apply here. The more sober framing: **months to low-single-digit years of consistent publishing**, not weeks.
- Given the case studies in Section 2 all describe *years* of publishing before monetisation became real (Latent Space: 2022 launch → meaningful scale reported in 2025; Stratechery: years to 40K paid; even fast-movers like The Neuron took roughly two years from founding to a life-changing acquisition), the realistic expectation for wAIbi-sabi is: **treat 2026–2027 as instrumentation-and-credibility-building, not monetisation, years.**

### 3.3 What to build first

Given the above, the sequencing that matches the evidence is:
1. **Analytics now** (near-zero cost, see Section 6) — you cannot manage what you don't measure, and right now there is no data at all.
2. **Newsletter capture mechanism now** (near-zero cost, see Section 5) — even at low traffic, every visitor who leaves without a way to resubscribe is a permanently lost future subscriber. This costs nothing to add and compounds.
3. **Traffic before monetisation, but subscriber capture in parallel with traffic** — don't wait for traffic to "arrive" before adding the list; the two should be built simultaneously since capture is nearly free.
4. **Consulting/credibility positioning is available immediately** and doesn't require any audience threshold — if the owner has expertise to sell, that door is open today, independent of blog metrics (see Simon Willison pattern).
5. **Sponsorship conversations are a 2027+ topic** at the earliest, contingent on steps 1–3 actually producing a list in the low thousands with a legible AI-native niche.

## 4. Sponsorship-editorial firewall precedents (how to take money without destroying credibility)

Since wAIbi-sabi's stated differentiator *is* trustworthiness (self-graded evidence, public corrections), the sponsorship model chosen later has to protect that asset. Three concrete precedents:

### 4.1 Wirecutter's "church and state" separation
- Editors pick products **based solely on quality**; the commerce/affiliate team is a **separate team that only gets involved after editorial picks are finalized**, deciding which affiliate links to use — editorial staff are described as unaware of the specific commercial agreements in place.
- Wirecutter **does not accept sponsored content, paid placements, or manufacturer incentives to influence picks** at all — affiliate revenue is decoupled entirely from which products get recommended.
- Crucially, when the *only* quality retailer for a recommended product has no affiliate program, Wirecutter sends readers there anyway and earns nothing — a costly but credibility-preserving policy that is worth stating explicitly as a public commitment.
- Source: [Affiverse: Wirecutter's case for radical transparency](https://www.affiversemedia.com/wirecutter-just-made-the-case-for-radical-transparency-most-publishers-wont-follow-it/)

### 4.2 Stratechery's no-ads, no-consulting-with-covered-companies rule
- Ben Thompson tried sponsored posts for six months early on, then dropped the model entirely in favor of subscriptions, explicitly to keep his **incentives aligned with readers rather than advertisers**.
- Separately, as of 2016 he committed to **not taking consulting or speaking arrangements with companies he covers editorially** — a direct, simple, publicly-stated conflict-of-interest rule.
- Source: [Everything-PR: Stratechery](https://everything-pr.com/ben-thompson-stratechery-original-solo-writer-operator), [The Tilt: Ben Thompson's ethics code](https://www.thetilt.com/content-entrepreneur/ben-thompson-stratechery)

### 4.3 Simon Willison's "sponsors get a banner, zero editorial influence" line
- His weekly sponsor placement is a text banner on the blog and a line at the top of the newsletter — **sponsors have no influence over editorial content**, and the ad unit is visually and structurally separated from the writing itself (this is the lightest-weight version of a firewall: physical/visual separation plus a stated no-influence rule, not a whole separate commerce team).
- This is the most directly copyable model for a solo blog with no separate business team: one clearly-labeled banner slot, a public one-line policy ("sponsors do not see or influence content before publication"), and otherwise business-as-usual editorial.
- Source: [Simon Willison's Newsletter stats/policy note](https://newsletterinsights.io/newsletter/simonw)

### 4.4 The Markup's structural approach (nonprofit, ad-free)
- The Markup runs **no advertisements, no paywalls, no third-party tracking**, and explicitly frames avoiding ad-tech/surveillance-capitalism revenue as necessary because it would conflict with the investigative journalism itself — funded by donations/grants instead.
- Less directly applicable to a for-profit-curious blog, but useful as the extreme end of the spectrum: **the only fully bulletproof firewall is not taking sponsorship money at all.**
- Source: [The Markup, via Wikipedia summary and about page](https://themarkup.org/about)

### 4.5 Recommendation for wAIbi-sabi specifically
Given the blog's stated differentiator is *grading its own evidence and publishing corrections publicly*, the cheapest and most on-brand precedent to adopt now (before any sponsor exists) is a hybrid of 4.2 and 4.3: **a short, public, dated policy page stating (a) sponsors get clearly labeled placement only, never influence over article content or evidence grading, (b) the blog will not accept sponsorship from a company whose product is the subject of an article being graded, and (c) any past or current sponsor relationship relevant to an article will be disclosed inline.** Writing this policy now — while there are zero sponsors — costs nothing and is itself a piece of the "radical honesty" content strategy (a transparency post about *how the blog plans to handle money before it has any* is exactly the kind of self-documenting content this blog already does well).

### 5.1 On the "no-expiring-credentials" constraint

This matters because the blog is run by an automated pipeline that needs to authenticate to whatever newsletter tool it uses without a human re-approving a token periodically. Buttondown's API uses a static API key (not an OAuth token requiring periodic re-consent), which fits a long-running automated pipeline better than services built around OAuth app installations that can be revoked or expire. Self-hosted listmonk avoids the question entirely since the pipeline would be talking to infrastructure the owner controls outright. This wasn't found as an explicitly documented comparison point across these tools in the search results above — it's an inference from how each API is architected, not a directly sourced claim, so it should be verified against current API docs before final tool selection.

### 5.2 Recommendation
**Buttondown** is the best starting point: free at zero-to-100 subscribers (matching current zero audience exactly), script/API-native by design, Markdown-first, and cheap to scale ($9/mo at 1,000 subscribers — a milestone that, per Section 3, is realistically months to a year-plus away). If the automated pipeline later needs zero ongoing cost at any scale and the owner is willing to run a small always-on process, **listmonk** is the credible free/self-hosted upgrade path.

### 6.1 On "cited but not visited" and AI-assistant referral visibility specifically

None of the free/cheap tools above natively separate "an AI assistant answered a question using my content and the reader never clicked through" from "no traffic happened." Cloudflare's AI Crawl Control / bot-traffic tooling is the only free mechanism found here that can show *AI bots reading the site* (both training crawlers like GPTBot/ClaudeBot and demand-driven "user" bots like ChatGPT-User/Claude-User/Perplexity-User that fetch a page live in response to a specific question) — this is a genuinely different signal from pageview analytics and worth having in addition to, not instead of, a normal analytics tool. Source: [Cloudflare Blog: AI crawler traffic by purpose and industry](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-crawler-traffic-by-purpose-and-industry/), [Cloudflare Blog: the crawl-to-click gap](https://blog.cloudflare.com/crawlers-click-ai-bots-training/).

### 6.2 Recommendation for wAIbi-sabi

**GoatCounter (hosted, free tier) as the primary analytics tool**, for three reasons: it's genuinely free forever at this traffic level (not a trial), it's the lightest script (matters for a blog whose whole ethos is minimal/honest, not bloated with third-party trackers), and it needs no infrastructure to run or maintain. Its weakness — no free-tier API for a routine to read programmatically — is a real gap given the pipeline is automated; if programmatic access to the numbers becomes a requirement, **Umami Cloud's free Hobby tier** (100K events/month, documented REST API, still genuinely free) is the better-fitting alternative and should probably be the actual first choice given the stated intent that "a routine will read the numbers." Layer **Cloudflare** in regardless (free, and the site can be proxied through Cloudflare DNS at zero cost even while staying on GitHub Pages) specifically to get visibility into AI-bot/crawler traffic, since that is the one signal plain pageview analytics cannot show and it's directly relevant to a blog whose audience may increasingly be "AI assistants reading it on a user's behalf."

## 7. "Being cited but not visited": what people are saying about what sponsors buy in that world (marked as speculation where appropriate)

**Established, sourced data (not speculation):**
- Roughly **60–68% of Google searches now end without a click**, and AI Overviews specifically reduce clicks to the top-ranking organic result by about **58%**. ([xseek: AI Answers traffic decline 2026](https://www.xseek.io/blogs/articles/ai-traffic-decline-2026), citing SparkToro/Similarweb data)
- Publisher-side, **median referral traffic from Google fell ~38% YoY** in some analyses, with health/finance/education verticals losing 11–23 percentage points of organic click share — this isn't uniform across niches. ([Superlines: AI Search Statistics 2026](https://www.superlines.io/articles/ai-search-statistics/))
- There is a documented **positive signal for citation itself**: brands/pages that get cited inside AI Overviews earn **35% more organic clicks and 91% more paid clicks** than non-cited pages/brands — being the *source* an AI cites correlates with more downstream traffic than not being cited at all, even though most AI answers overall don't generate a click to anyone. ### 7.1 Implication for wAIbi-sabi, stated plainly (analysis, not sourced claim)

If wAIbi-sabi's content gets cited by an AI assistant rather than clicked, standard pageview-based sponsor pitches ("we get X thousand monthly visitors") won't capture that value at all — the value would be in *being a trusted source AI systems draw from*, which is unmeasurable with today's free analytics tools except indirectly via the Cloudflare AI-bot-traffic signal described in Section 6.1. For a radical-honesty AI blog specifically, "our content is verifiably accurate enough that AI assistants cite it, and here's the bot-traffic log proving it happens" could eventually become a distinctive pitch to AI companies — closer to a credibility/brand-association sell than a reach sell. This lines up with the consulting/credibility model (Section 1.4, 2) more than the sponsorship-CPM model (Section 1.1, 2), because credibility-based value doesn't require pageview scale, while CPM-based sponsorship explicitly does. This is a reasoned inference from the sourced data above, not itself a sourced claim — flagged here as the report's own analysis.
